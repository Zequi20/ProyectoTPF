    Measuring a fish with a camera would spare it from being taken out of the water, but photographing it through a tank introduces an error: light bends as it crosses water, glass and air, and the apparent size of the fish changes with its distance from the glass, which a single image does not reveal. The objective was to compare four ways of converting pixels to millimetres on the same anatomical points---the scale of an ArUco marker attached to the glass (A), a homography from a marker in the plane of the replica (\Aprime), a pinhole calibration performed under water (B) and a flat-port refractive model (C)---and to quantify the length interval left by an unknown depth. We hypothesised that C would have the lowest error, that its advantage over B would grow away from the calibration zone, and that its interval would contain the true length. We photographed six rigid PLA replicas, measured with a calliper, at four depths (5, 11, 17 and 23 cm), three times each (72 images). With known depth, B and C achieved the lowest total-length errors (MAE 1.08 mm, 0.73\,\%, and 1.22 mm, 0.88\,\%), followed by \Aprime{} (3.29 mm) and A (37.43 mm); both outperformed A and \Aprime{} in all six replicas (sign test, $p = 0.031$) and did not differ from each other ($p = 1$). The first hypothesis held only against A and \Aprime, the second did not, and the third did: the interval contained the calliper length in all 72 captures. The error of A grew from 13.8 to 57.6 mm as the replica moved away from the glass, as geometry predicts. With unknown depth, the interval spans 56 to 82\,\% of the length; bounding depth to $\pm 1$~cm reduces it to 4 to 5\,\%. We conclude that, with known depth, both the underwater calibration and the refractive model bring the error below 1\,\%; without it, the main uncertainty is depth, not refraction. The results apply to this setup and to rigid replicas.
