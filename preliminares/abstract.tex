    Measuring a fish with a camera would spare it from being taken out of the water, but photographing it through the wall of a tank introduces a source of error that a single camera cannot resolve: light bends as it crosses water, glass and air, and the apparent size of the fish changes with its distance from the glass, which the image does not reveal. In a controlled setup, we tested four ways of converting pixels to millimetres on the same anatomical points: a scale taken from an ArUco marker attached to the glass (A), a homography from a marker in the plane of the replica (\Aprime), an effective pinhole calibration performed under water (B), and an explicit flat-port refractive model (C). We photographed six rigid PLA replicas measured with a calliper at four depths (5, 11, 17 and 23 cm), with three captures per condition (72 images). With known depth, B and C achieved the lowest total-length errors (MAE 1.08 mm, 0.73\,\%, and 1.22 mm, 0.88\,\%), followed by \Aprime{} (3.29 mm; 2.28\,\%) and A (37.43 mm; 25.68\,\%); both outperformed A and \Aprime{} in all six replicas (sign test by replica, $p = 0.031$) and did not differ from each other ($p = 1$). B did not degrade away from its calibration zone. The error of A grew from 13.8 to 57.6 mm as the replica moved away from the glass, just as first-order geometry predicts. The same geometry shows that, when depth is unknown, the length is confined to an interval spanning 56 to 82\,\% of its value, which shrinks to 4 to 5\,\% when depth is known to within $\pm 1$~cm. We conclude that, through a flat glass and with known depth, both the underwater calibration and the refractive model bring the conversion error below 1\,\%; without that information, the main source of uncertainty is depth, not refraction. The results are limited to the setup, the rigid replicas and the optical conditions used.
