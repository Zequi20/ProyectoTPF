\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\begin{otherlanguage}{english}
Image-based fish morphometry avoids handling the animal, but when the camera looks through the wall of a tank, refraction at the air--glass--water interface changes the apparent scale with the depth of the fish, and a single camera cannot recover that depth. This work evaluated, in a controlled setup, four strategies for converting pixels to millimetres applied to the same anatomical points: a direct scale from an ArUco marker fixed to the glass (A), a homography from a coplanar marker (\Aprime), an effective pinhole calibration performed under water (B), and an explicit flat-port refractive model (C). Six rigid PLA replicas measured with a calliper, four depths (5, 11, 17 and 23 cm) and three captures per condition were used, for a total of 72 images and 24 replica--depth combinations. The four points were marked manually once per image and reused by all four methods. With known depth, C achieved the lowest total-length error (MAE 1.22 mm; 0.88\,\%), followed by \Aprime{} (3.29 mm; 2.28\,\%), B (16.32 mm; 11.24\,\%) and A (37.43 mm; 25.68\,\%), and was the best method in all six replicas and all 24 replica--depth combinations (sign test by replica, $p = 0.031$). The error of A grew from 13.8 to 57.6 mm as the replica moved away from the glass, in agreement with a first-order geometric prediction. With the same geometry, the length interval induced by an unknown depth spans 56 to 82\,\% of the length when depth is unconstrained, and 4 to 5\,\% when depth is known to within $\pm 1$~cm. It is concluded that, through a flat glass, the refractive model reduces the conversion error below 1\,\% and that depth, rather than refraction, is the dominant source of uncertainty when it is unknown. The results are limited to the setup, the rigid replicas and the optical conditions used.
