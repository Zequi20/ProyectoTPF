\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\begin{otherlanguage}{english}
Image-based fish morphometry avoids handling the animal, but when the camera looks through the wall of a tank, refraction at the air--glass--water interface changes the apparent scale with the depth of the fish, and a single camera cannot recover that depth. This work evaluated, in a controlled setup, four strategies for converting pixels to millimetres applied to the same anatomical points: a direct scale from an ArUco marker fixed to the glass (A), a homography from a coplanar marker (\Aprime), an effective pinhole calibration performed under water (B), and an explicit flat-port refractive model (C). Six rigid PLA replicas measured with a calliper, four depths (5, 11, 17 and 23 cm) and three captures per condition were used (72 images). With known depth, B and C achieved the lowest total-length errors (MAE 1.08 mm, 0.73\,\%, and 1.22 mm, 0.88\,\%), followed by \Aprime{} (3.29 mm; 2.28\,\%) and A (37.43 mm; 25.68\,\%); both outperformed A and \Aprime{} in all six replicas (sign test by replica, $p = 0.031$) and did not differ from each other ($p = 1$). B did not degrade away from its calibration zone. The error of A grew from 13.8 to 57.6 mm as the replica moved away from the glass, in agreement with a first-order geometric prediction. With the same geometry, the length interval induced by an unknown depth spans 56 to 82\,\% of the length when depth is unconstrained, and 4 to 5\,\% when depth is known to within $\pm 1$~cm. It is concluded that, through a flat glass and with known depth, both the underwater calibration and the refractive model reduce the conversion error below 1\,\%, and that depth, rather than refraction, is the dominant source of uncertainty when it is unknown. The results are limited to the setup, the rigid replicas and the optical conditions used.
